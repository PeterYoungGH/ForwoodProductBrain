## Abstract / Introduction

Advancement of safety requires a standardised method of measuring and communicating safety performance. A common safety metric enables professionals across industries to compare outcomes, assess trends, and make strategic decisions. Although never explicitly intended as a comparative safety metric, Total Recordable Incident Rate (TRIR) has been the dominant indicator of safety performance for more than 50 years. Defined as the rate at which a company experiences an OSHA-recordable incident scaled per 200,000 work hours, TRIR has been widely used for contractor prequalification, incentives, and performance evaluation.

Despite its simplicity and ubiquity, recent research has demonstrated that TRIR has serious statistical and philosophical limitations that impede strategic decision-making and long-term improvement. This has led safety professionals to question: *If not TRIR, then what?*

Leading indicators are often proposed as an alternative, but these too suffer from a lack of consistent definition, standardisation, and benchmarking capability. As a result, there remains a need for a safety assessment method that:

1. Enables consistent and objective assessment of safety related to working conditions at any point in time.
2. Statistically explains the relationship between safety activities and long-term injury outcomes.

This paper introduces **High-Energy Control Assessment (HECA)** as a new approach to monitoring and measuring safety performance. HECA is grounded in modern safety science, human and organisational performance principles, and focuses on the presence of safeguards against serious injury and fatality (SIF) risks.

---

## Background: Limitations of TRIR

The quality of any performance metric can be evaluated against six criteria: objective, valid, predictive, clear, actionable, and important.

### Evaluation of TRIR

* **Objective:** TRIR is objective in that it is based on recorded incidents, though subject to underreporting and manipulation.
* **Valid:** TRIR is not statistically valid for short-term or comparative use. Analysis of trillions of worker hours shows injuries are largely random events, requiring extremely large datasets to be statistically meaningful.
* **Predictive:** TRIR does not predict future injuries or fatalities and has no demonstrated relationship with SIFs.
* **Clear:** TRIR is easy to understand and communicate.
* **Actionable:** TRIR does not support proactive or targeted intervention.
* **Important:** TRIR reflects an outdated view of safety as the absence of injury, rather than the presence of safeguards, and does not focus on SIF prevention.

The conclusion is that while TRIR is simple and familiar, it is fundamentally misaligned with contemporary safety thinking.

## What Is HECA?

HECA is defined as the **percentage of high-energy hazards with a corresponding direct control**.

Each observation is classified as either:

* **Success:** A high-energy hazard has a corresponding direct control.
* **Exposure:** A high-energy hazard does not have a corresponding direct control.

### HECA Formula

HECA = Successes / (Successes + Exposures)

HECA is binary at the observation level and is designed to be collected during active work.

---

## High-Energy Hazards

High-energy hazards are defined as hazards involving more than **1,500 joules of physical energy**, as these are most likely to result in serious injury or fatality.

Examples include:

* Suspended loads
* Mobile equipment and traffic
* Heavy rotating equipment
* Electrical energy (>50V)
* Pressure and explosions
* High-temperature sources

Energy-based classification shifts focus from worst-case possibilities to the most likely severe outcomes, improving prioritisation and prevention of SIFs.

---

## Direct Controls

A **direct control** must meet all three criteria:

1. **Targeted** to the specific high-energy hazard.
2. **Effectively mitigates or eliminates** the hazard energy to below the SIF threshold when properly installed, verified, and used.
3. **Effective despite unintentional human error** during work execution.

Examples of direct controls include engineered fall protection, machine guarding, lockout/tagout, trench boxes, and de-energised electrical systems.

Controls reliant on perfect human behaviour (e.g. signage, training alone, situational awareness) are not considered direct controls.

---

## HECA Case Example

A pipe shack lifting operation identified nine high-energy hazards and six low-energy hazards. Each high-energy hazard was assessed for the presence of a corresponding direct control.

* 5 hazards had direct controls (successes)
* 4 hazards did not (exposures)

Resulting HECA score:

**HECA = 5 / 9 = 56%**

This indicates that 56% of identified high-energy hazards were adequately controlled.

---

## HECA as Monitoring (Not Leading or Lagging)

HECA is positioned between traditional leading and lagging indicators:

* It is **not a leading indicator**, as it does not measure activities.
* It is **not a lagging indicator**, as it does not rely on incidents.

Instead, HECA functions as a **monitoring variable**, enabling near real-time insight into safety system effectiveness before serious incidents occur.

---

## Operationalising HECA

HECA can be integrated into existing safety observation programs. Recommended data capture includes:

* High-energy hazards observed
* Presence or absence of direct controls
* Types of direct controls present
* Controls missing where exposure exists

This enables both tactical intervention and strategic analysis.

---

## Intelligence and Analysis from HECA

HECA supports:

* Trend analysis over time
* Comparison across business units
* Identification of poorly controlled hazard categories
* Targeted investment in missing controls
* Organisational learning and resource allocation

Visualisations can show trends by hazard type, control type, location, or business unit.

---

## Evaluation of HECA Against Metric Criteria

* **Objective:** High potential, though some observer judgement remains.
* **Valid:** Can be collected in high volume, enabling statistical stability.
* **Predictive:** Not yet empirically proven.
* **Clear:** Moderately clear; requires training.
* **Important:** Strong alignment with modern safety principles and SIF prevention.
* **Actionable:** Supports proactive decision-making and capacity building.

---

## Conclusions and Recommendations

HECA represents a shift toward safety metrics aligned with contemporary safety science. For successful adoption:

* A single, shared definition of HECA must be maintained.
* HECA should be used for learning and improvement, not comparison or incentives.
* Operational guidance, sampling strategies, and validation are required.
* Relationships between leading indicators, HECA, and lagging indicators should be empirically explored.

HECA offers a promising, intentionally designed approach to safety performance monitoring that focuses on the presence of safeguards and enables continuous learning and strategic decision-making.
