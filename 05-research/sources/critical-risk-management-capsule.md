## Summary

This research captures foundational domain knowledge about Critical Risk Management (CRM), including core concepts such as the distinction between hazards and risks, the 14 critical risk categories, and the five-step CRM process. This knowledge is essential for understanding how Forwood's CRM methodology works and informs product development, training, and customer communication.

---

## Key Points

- Critical risks are hazards capable of causing serious, permanent harm or death
- Hazards and risks are distinct concepts: hazards describe potential sources of harm, while risks describe the probability of harm occurring
- The Swiss Cheese Model illustrates how multiple layers of defense can fail when gaps align
- Critical Risk Management focuses on 14 categories of high-potential hazards
- Effective CRM follows a structured five-step process: Planning, Identification, Estimation, Implementation, and Follow-up

---

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Hazards vs Risks Distinction
The document clearly distinguishes between hazards (potential sources of damage) and risks (probability of harm when exposed to a hazard). This distinction is fundamental to understanding CRM methodology and is often confused in practice. The example of a busy road as a hazard, with varying risk levels depending on conditions, illustrates this concept effectively.

### Finding 2: The 14 Critical Risks
Critical Risk Management focuses on 14 specific categories of high-potential hazards:
1. Confined spaces
2. Entanglement or collapse
3. Unplanned explosive initiation
4. Falling from height
5. Vehicle and pedestrian collision
6. Slope failure
7. Vehicle collision and rollover
8. Electrical hazards
9. Hoisting operations
10. Exposure to hazardous substances
11. Drowning
12. Falling objects
13. Uncontrolled energy release
14. Fire and dangerous atmosphere (including underground environments)

Each category requires tailored control measures suited to its specific risk profile.

### Finding 3: Five-Step CRM Process
Effective critical risk management follows a structured approach:
1. **Planning**: Define tasks and assign responsibilities
2. **Identification**: Inventory and identify all critical risks present
3. **Estimation**: Evaluate the level of each identified risk
4. **Implementation**: Apply suitable control measures
5. **Follow-up**: Monitor and verify controls remain effective

This process ensures systematic risk management from planning through ongoing verification.

### Finding 4: Control Measure Limitations
Control measures are never 100% effective—gaps can occur through oversights, lack of diligence, or failures in maintenance or procedure. When an accident occurs, it means all preventive measures failed. This understanding is critical for designing robust safety systems that account for human and system failures.
