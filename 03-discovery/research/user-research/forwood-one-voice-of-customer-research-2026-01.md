## Research Question

Who are our customers as people, what do they struggle with, what are they hoping for, and what do they hope to achieve with a solution like ours? What messaging will resonate most strongly with our target market?

## Objectives

- Understand customer personas and their lived experiences
- Identify pain points, struggles, and hopes from real customer feedback
- Document buyer psychology for our 3 target personas
- Extract messaging insights for demand gen, marketing, sales conversations, and product development
- Understand the transformation customers experience after using Forwood

## Methodology

### Research Method
Voice of Customer Research - Comprehensive synthesis of:
- Raw messaging mining from target market
- VoC from customer interviews and recordings
- VoC research from adjacent "buyers"
- Real feedback from real people with lived experience

### Participants/Data Sources
- Primary persona: Safety System Owner (HSE Director / Safety Director / Risk Leader / Sustainability Lead)
- Secondary persona: Accountable Executive (CEO / COO / EVP / Board / Asset Owner)
- Tertiary persona: Operational Leader (Superintendents, Supervisors, Site Leaders)
- General target market feedback
- Consultant segment feedback
- Customer interviews and recordings
- Market research and industry forums

## Key Findings

### Finding 1: Primary Persona - Accountability Without Authority

**What they're experiencing:** EHS professionals are responsible for preventing fatalities but cannot control incentives, schedules, or production pressure. They're blamed when they push too hard AND when incidents happen. They're caught between leadership expectations, operational resistance, and personal ethics with no real authority.

**Why this is acute for buyers:** This is the daily lived reality driving purchase decisions. They're looking for tools that give them leverage and credibility they can't generate alone. They need systems that make the problem visible to those who DO have authority.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "You're not doing enough, but please back off"
- "Almost got fired for enforcing LOTO"
- "I don't report to anyone in the building. I have a dotted line to the general manager and in some ways I see how disconnected they are"

**Frequency:** Extremely high - this underlying tension appears across multiple threads and contexts.

### Finding 2: Primary Persona - Drowning in Data, Starving for Action

**What they're experiencing:** Overloaded with data collection, analysis, dashboards, and reporting that never drives decisions or changes. Forced into data analyst roles they're often not trained for, when they should be managing safety. Leadership asks for "more data" but nothing happens with it. Charts are performative.

**Why this is acute for buyers:** They're exhausted by systems that create busy-work without impact. They want tools that connect data to action, not just more reporting. The gap between information and impact is killing their effectiveness and morale.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "What I've seen is a lot of data collection, lots of discussion, and no action"
- "Have any of you been hired into a safety role, to only transform into a data analyst role? ...I have absolutely no bandwidth for that and we are suffering"
- "lots of data, no action... fancy charts... what are you actually doing with it"

**Frequency:** Very high - appears in multiple dedicated threads with significant engagement.

### Finding 3: Primary Persona - Safety Theatre vs Real Risk

**What they're experiencing:** Existing systems produce checklists, dashboards, compliance artifacts - but don't reliably prevent serious injury or death. Strong resentment toward "paper safety" and audit-driven work. They see focus on minor issues (PPE, rolled ankles) while critical risks persist.

**Why this is acute for buyers:** They know their current systems are performative and it eats at them morally. They're looking for solutions that address fatal risk, not just satisfy audits. They want to do meaningful work, not compliance theatre.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "Often there is a focus on small events and not critical risks. As a manager, I am much more interested in getting learnings out of a near miss than a rolled ankle"
- "checklist, don't care how it gets done"
- "Oilfield safety basically means fill out that checklist. Don't care how it gets done"

**Frequency:** Very high - strong emotional intensity across multiple contexts.

### Finding 4: Primary Persona - Professional Burnout and Career Exits

**What they're experiencing:** There's deep emotional exhaustion in our primary segment. They feel undervalued, isolated, and stuck in a thankless role. They're caught in impossible position with no structural support. Many are actively planning exits or role changes. Describe job as "soul sucking" and feeling like they're "banging head against wall."

**Why this is acute for buyers:** They're at breaking point and evaluating whether to stay in the profession. They need solutions that make the job sustainable and give them wins. Tools that help them feel effective rather than futile. This is about professional survival.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "I am feeling incredibly burnt out though. I dread going to work. I feel like I've lost my drive. I'm feeling bitter about how thankless my job is"
- "Get out while the getting good. I'm 36... I've been hating the job since I've been your age... This job is soul sucking"
- "I am in the same boat. I have cashed it in and have demanded a transfer to a new role or I am resigning come spring time. I am tired of coming to work and banging my head against the wall"

**Frequency:** High - multiple long, detailed threads with significant community resonance.

### Finding 5: Primary Persona - Credibility Erosion - Seen as Police, Not Partners

**What they're experiencing:** Workers don't trust them. Operations sees them as obstacles. Leadership uses them as scapegoats. Lack of credibility comes from being associated with impractical rules, blame-focused systems, and lack of follow-through. Described as creating "atmosphere of distrust." Safety professionals become messengers for decisions they didn't make.

**Why this is acute for buyers:** Their effectiveness depends on trust and they've lost it. They need systems that position them as enabling partners, not compliance cops. They want to rebuild relationships and be seen as genuinely helping workers.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "bad safety professionals have created an atmosphere of distrust, by acting with unearned authority"
- "a good safety professional in construction must be good at communicating and making crews understand WHY safety is important and that you genuinely care for their well being"
- "when I show up at our different buildings there's always someone who says 'Whoa, where have you been' when I was there yesterday, or simply leave the room when I walk in"

**Frequency:** High - appears across multiple contexts and industries.

### Finding 6: Primary Persona - Systems That Look Good, But Perform Poorly

**What they're experiencing:** Frustration with expensive EHS platforms (Enablon, INX, etc.) that are bloated, admin-heavy, compliance-oriented, and hated by practitioners. Software adoption constrained by governance, redundancy checks, and change management. Stuck using Excel because it's familiar and auditable.

**Why this is acute for buyers:** They've been burned by previous "solutions" that created more work without solving problems and are deeply sceptical of new systems. They need proof that tools will actually work in the field and won't become another layer of bureaucracy.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "My company chose Enablon before I was hired in. If I had the choice between using enablon every day for the rest of my life or nail gunning my scrotum to a 2x4 I would really struggle with that choice"
- "it takes a long time to get software approved. We need to make sure the software is not redundant with another piece we already own"
- "Regulation mostly for me. A lot of my work need to be able to be audited and reviewed by outside parties, so I am stuck using Excel"

**Frequency:** Moderate-high - strong emotional reactions when mentioned.

### Finding 7: Primary Persona - Controls That Satisfy Audits But Don't Prevent Harm

**What they're experiencing:** EHS professionals are implementing risk assessments and controls that prioritize audit defensibility over real-world effectiveness. They're forced to accept administrative controls (spotters, signage, training) for fatal risks when engineering solutions are needed. They KNOW these controls don't meaningfully reduce harm - and when incidents happen, they're blamed for "inadequate controls."

**Why this is acute for buyers:** This creates moral injury and professional liability. They're accountable for preventing fatalities using tools they don't believe work. They need systems that help them identify and advocate for controls that actually work under real conditions - and give them evidence to push back on superficial solutions.

**Evidence:**
- "the identified consequence could realistically result in a fatality, yet the proposed control was simply to assign a spotter. But is that really an effective control, or have we just given someone a front-row seat to a serious incident?"
- "Dumbing this down isn't the solution. If anything, it's making the problem worse"

**Frequency:** High - strong emotional intensity, particularly from experienced EHS professionals who've seen the pattern repeat.

### Finding 8: General Market - Non-standardisation Inconsistency Creates Real Risk

**What we heard:** There is deep frustration with the absence of shared standards across sites and companies, even for basic, high-frequency safety activities. For people who move across sites, safety is experienced less as protection and more as constant context-switching, where remembering "this site's version" of the same risk becomes a liability in itself.

**Why it matters:** The sentiment is not "we want fewer rules," but "the inconsistency is dangerous". Workers explicitly link lack of standardisation to deaths, serious injuries, and unnecessary cognitive load.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "In the event of an actual issue what are the odds you will remember the right processes for this site"
- "the difference in priority rules has killed 2 people in WA the last couple of years... an industry standard would have prevented those 2 deaths"

**Market reality:** Safety becomes constant context-switching and site-specific dialects of the same risks.

### Finding 9: General Market - Safety Systems Exist to Protect the Company, Not Workers

**What we heard:** A strong and emotionally charged belief that safety systems are pre-wired for corporate deniability and blame transfer. Many contributors describe safety processes as pre-arranged mechanisms to assign blame downward when something goes wrong, with workers expecting to be "thrown under the bus."

**Why it matters:** This perception erodes trust: even when safety actions might help workers, they are interpreted through a lens of defensive bureaucracy and deniability. It explains withheld reporting, procedural cynicism, and pre-emptive distrust of safety messaging.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "The safety department's sole responsibility is to keep the company safe. If it also happens to keep you safe this is just an overlap. They will throw you under the bus at the first opportunity"
- "safety policies and procedures are purely to keep liability away from the company, everything else is a bonus"

**Market reality:** Workers view safety systems as legal shields and blame mechanisms.

### Finding 10: General Market - Safety is Conditional - Production Wins When It Matters

**What we heard:** Safety compliance is enforced when it's easy, visible, or bureaucratic. When safety requires stopping work, spending money, or delaying output, it's quietly deprioritised. Workers learn actual priorities by watching what gets rewarded, not what gets said.

**Why it matters:** Creates learned scepticism where people focus on low-consequence issues (PPE, test-n-tags) while critical risks persist. The gap between stated values and observed behaviour destroys credibility.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "Safety before Production, until Safety impacts Production"
- "They only care when it's easy... if you're going to hold me up about my safety, lets talk for real! ... Suddenly everything's ok and they have to be somewhere else"

**Market reality:** Workers watch production override safety "when it matters" and adjust behaviour accordingly.

### Finding 11: General Market - Reporting Goes Into a Black Hole

**What we heard:** People don't withhold reports because they don't care - they withhold them because reporting leads to no action, blame, extra work, or reputational risk. The #1 complaint is that reports "go straight in the bin" with no visible follow-through.

**Why it matters:** This reframes reporting as a trust mechanism, not a data mechanism. Explains persistent under-reporting more convincingly than fear alone.

**Quotes from the market:**
- "the number 1 complaint I get about reporting is employees feel like their reports go directly in the bin and nothing gets fixed"
- "What I've seen is a lot of data collection, lots of discussion, and no action"

**Market reality:** Workers have learned that speaking up changes nothing, so they stop speaking.

### Finding 12: Executive Persona - SIF Risk Has Become a Board-Owned Governance Obligation

**High-level synthesis:** Serious injury and fatality (SIF) prevention is no longer viewed as an operational or HSE responsibility alone. Regulatory reform (e.g. industrial manslaughter, due-diligence duties) has explicitly repositioned fatal risk as a board-level accountability. Executives are expected to demonstrate personal understanding, active oversight, and evidence-based governance of fatal risks. Delegation is no longer a defence.

**What this reveals:**
- Safety is now inseparable from enterprise risk governance.
- Boards are being judged not on intent, but on whether they can prove they knew their fatal risks and acted accordingly.

### Finding 13: Executive Persona - Traditional Safety Metrics Mask Fatal Risk Exposure

**High-level synthesis:** Lagging indicators (TRIFR, LTIFR) can improve while fatal risk exposure remains unchanged. Boards often receive large volumes of safety data that lack relevance to SIF risk. Executives struggle to see early warning signals for catastrophic events, despite "strong" safety reporting.

**What this reveals:**
- Executives are not under-informed, they are misinformed.
- The gap is not data availability, but risk signal quality at governance level.

### Finding 14: Executive Persona - Paper Compliance Is a Legal and Reputational Liability

**High-level synthesis:** Courts, regulators, and public scrutiny increasingly distinguish between systems that exist on paper, and systems that demonstrably work in the field. Post-incident reviews focus on whether critical controls were implemented, verified, and resourced, not whether policies existed. "Reasonably practicable" is being interpreted through evidence of real control effectiveness.

**What this reveals:**
- Compliance theatre increases executive exposure rather than reducing it.
- Executives are at risk precisely when systems look complete but are not operationally reliable.

### Finding 15: Executive Persona - Fatal Risk Governance Breaks Down Across Complex Operations

**High-level synthesis:** High-risk organisations operate across multiple sites, jurisdictions, contractors and joint ventures. Each layer introduces variation in risk maturity, incentives, and execution. Boards struggle to obtain a coherent, consolidated view of fatal risk across the enterprise.

**What this reveals:**
- Fragmentation is a governance risk in itself.
- Without a unifying SIF framework, executives cannot confidently assert control over fatal risk exposure.

### Finding 16: Executive Persona - Leadership Credibility Directly Affects Risk Exposure

**High-level synthesis:** Executive behaviour sends stronger signals than formal policy. When production pressure overrides safety commitments, safety climate degrades and SIF exposure rises. Conversely, visible leadership engagement (asking the right questions, backing stop-work decisions) correlates with stronger reporting and control adherence.

**What this reveals:**
- Culture is not abstract; it is the cumulative effect of executive decisions and trade-offs.
- Boards are increasingly judged on whether leadership actions are consistent with safety rhetoric.

### Finding 17: Customer VoC - Fatality Prevention is the Organising Principle

**What customers are saying:** Explicit differentiation between minor injuries (rolled ankles, short-term harm) and fatalities/life-altering injuries with irreversible ripple effects. This distinction is moral, emotional, and organisational - not abstract. Fatality prevention cuts through debate and focuses attention.

**Why it matters:** Provides clarity of purpose in a cluttered, contested space. "Zero harm" is treated as noisy; fatality prevention is concrete and grounding.

**Evidence:**
- "if someone rolls their ankle and they're off work a couple days... that's not good, but that ankle's going to get better. If we critically injure or kill somebody, the ripple effect is never-ending"
- "the idea of a program that's focused specifically on fatality prevention is what we wanted"
- "not what could hurt you but really shifting that mindset to what could kill you"

### Finding 18: Customer VoC - Systems Not People - Human Error is Inevitable

**What customers are saying:** Consistent shift away from personal decision-making, rule-following, and individual blame. Toward system design, control effectiveness, and how work is planned and executed. Human error is treated as inevitable; system failure is addressable.

**Why it matters:** Reflects mature view of risk - people aren't the problem to be fixed, systems are. Values approaches that acknowledge human fallibility without moral judgment.

**Evidence:**
- "we're all human and we're all going to make mistakes. So if we can look at a better system to execute work, and higher levels of controls, that's what CRM's about"
- "really helped us put an emphasis on overall systems and performance and not necessarily personal decision making"

### Finding 19: Customer VoC - Credibility Depends on Visible, Consistent Leadership

**What customers are saying:** Culture change impossible without leaders being physically present, using same language consistently, acting in alignment with stated values. Credibility is fragile - one tolerated unsafe act undoes progress. Statistics alone don't work; personal, values-based engagement matters.

**Why it matters:** Workers detect culture, they don't accept declarations. Hypocrisy kills credibility instantly.

**Evidence:**
- "Walking the talk. You can't say one thing and do something else"
- "If you lose credibility when it comes to safety and critical risk management, and you condone an unsafe act, you've just lost all that credibility"
- "if it's all about statistics it won't hit home... But if you can make personal connections with people"

### Finding 20: Customer VoC - Non-Blame Culture Enables Truth

**What customers are saying:** Non-blame approach credited with increased openness, better conversations, more honest reporting. Especially important in high power-distance cultures where speaking up is risky. Blame actively suppresses needed information.

**Why it matters:** People want to contribute to safety but only when safe to do so. Absence of blame isn't softness - it's prerequisite for truth.

**Evidence:**
- "Because it's a system that does not involved with blame, it's more about leaning in and finding out why your critical controls may be missing... employees opening up"
- "distance in the power dynamic between supervisors and management to employees"

### Finding 21: Customer VoC - Stop-Work Authority Requires Top-Down Permission

**What customers are saying:** Ability for workers to stop work is a mindset change, not policy change. Only becomes real when leaders visibly support it and issues are celebrated rather than punished. CEO-level advocacy essential to legitimise behaviour.

**Why it matters:** Workers already understand risk - what they need is permission. Celebrating problems signals seriousness more than hiding them.

**Evidence:**
- "biggest mindshift is enabling the worker to stop the work... started at the CEO pushing the program"
- "Celebrating the red, celebrating the issues, instead of it being a bad thing"
- "making workers comfortable stopping the work until the correct controls are in place"

### Finding 22: Customer VoC - Front-End Planning is Highest-Leverage Investment

**What customers are saying:** Planning, engineering, and design are most effective places to eliminate critical risk. Acknowledged this feels like cost, delays production, lacks immediate output. Yet seen as paying for itself through fewer catastrophic events.

**Why it matters:** Reflects long-term, systems-thinking mindset. Willingness to trade short-term efficiency for long-term reliability.

**Evidence:**
- "the biggest bang for buck is on the planning side... it's a cost without necessarily tangible results, but it pays for itself, because fewer mistakes are going to be made"
- "If you can design out hazards and look at your methodology, and eliminate critical risk through design"

### Finding 23: The Forwood Transformation - Before and After

**The Transformation Our Customers Experience:**

| Before (Market/EHS Reality) | After (What CRM+ Changed) |
|----------------------------|---------------------------|
| Leadership absent from field | Leaders regularly in field engaging on controls |
| Blame-driven → reporting suppressed | Non-blame → employees opening up |
| Hazard-counting and minor injuries | Fatal-risk focus - "what could kill you" |
| Stop-work theoretical/punished | Stop-work authority real and celebrated |
| Issues hidden or ignored | Issues celebrated and discussed openly |
| Data overload, no insight | Data centralized and actionable |
| Inconsistent language/approach | Consistent language across all levels |
| Individual blame for failures | System-level analysis and improvement |

**Evidence-Based Transformations:**
- **Leadership in field** → "One of the key impacts we've seen is, having leaders out in the field on a more regular basis"
- **Non-blame opens communication** → "Because it's a system that does not involved with blame... employees opening up"
- **Flipped from hazards to fatal risks** → "we've kind of flipped the paradigm upside down... now we're really focused on things that can actually really kill you"
- **Stop-work authority became real** → "enabling the worker to stop the work... started at the CEO pushing the program"
- **Celebrating issues instead of hiding** → "Celebrating the red, celebrating the issues"
- **Data becomes useful** → "getting that data in one place and getting it out and making it useful, is the advantage"
- **Consistent language across org** → "same language, behaviour and engagement across the different levels"
- **Systems vs people focus** → "emphasis on overall systems and performance and not necessarily personal decision making"

### Finding 24: Critical Observation - Market vs Customer VoC Contrast

**The market VoC is dominated by:**
- scepticism
- defensiveness
- fatigue
- learned distrust.

**The customer VoC is dominated by:**
- clarity
- alignment
- confidence
- long-term thinking.

**Key Insight:** Customers are not naïve or uncritical. After using Forwood, many of the ambient tensions present in the market have been reduced enough that people can think systemically instead of defensively.

## Insights

Synthesized insights from the research:

### Primary Persona Psychology Insight

**What emerges clearly:** EHS professionals are looking for systems that give them leverage, credibility, and impact - not more administrative burden.

**They need tools that:**
- Make fatal risk visible to leadership (solves authority gap)
- Connect data to action (solves meaningless busy-work)
- Focus on what matters (solves theatre problem)
- Make them effective (solves burnout/sustainability)
- Position them as enablers (solves credibility problem)
- Actually work in reality (solves failed-system fatigue)

**The buying question underneath everything:** "Will this finally help me do what I'm accountable for - or is it another system I have to manage that won't change outcomes?"

**What this persona is actually trying to solve:**
- prevent catastrophic events in systems they do not fully control
- create safety that survives production pressure, leadership turnover, contractor variability
- stop being the shock absorber between human failure and organisational denial

### Executive Persona - The Leadership SIF Prevention Paradox

**On the ground, operators are experiencing:**
- Leaders say safety matters
- Leaders reward production
- Stop-work is tolerated until it costs time or money
- SIF prevention loses when schedules tighten

**The key distinction executives live with (but rarely articulate):**
Executives are not choosing safety vs productivity. They're balancing low-frequency, high-consequence, abstract future harm, with immediate, visible, and measurable production losses.

**Why leadership behaviour drifts toward production:**
1. Executives lack credible signals of rising SIF risk
2. Leadership incentives are misaligned by system design
3. Executives underestimate how their behaviour propagates

**Recommended Messaging:**
"Most organisations unintentionally optimise for productivity because they cannot see fatal-risk degradation in time."

"We make those risks visible early enough to govern them without stopping the business unnecessarily."

### Persona Psychology Breakdown

**Safety System Owner (Primary):**
- **Jobs To Be Done:** Build and run a safety system that actually controls fatal risks in real work, not just on paper
- **Core Pain Points:** Accountability without authority, Safety theatre vs real risk, Signal lost in noise, Credibility erosion, Burnout and role drift
- **Core Motivators:** Preventing irreversible harm, Systemic control not heroics, Credibility and consistency, Leverage with leadership
- **Dream State:** Fatal risk is visible and prioritized, Safety survives production pressure, Data drives action not reports, Professional sustainability

**Accountable Executive (Co-Buyer):**
- **Jobs To Be Done:** Ensure the organization can reliably prevent fatal incidents and demonstrate due diligence when it matters
- **Core Pain Points:** False confidence from "green dashboards", Personal exposure they cannot delegate, Lack of line-of-sight from boardroom to field, Credibility gap between words and reality
- **Core Motivators:** Preventing irreversible harm on their watch, Protecting personal and organizational legitimacy, Confidence not optimism (evidence-based)
- **Dream State:** Board-level confidence in fatal-risk control, Small number of meaningful signals, Direct evidence controls work under pressure

**Operational Leader (Adopter):**
- **Jobs To Be Done:** Execute work safely while meeting production/schedule commitments
- **Core Pain Points:** Conflicting incentives (safety vs production), Unclear priorities - too many rules, Being set up to fail, Distrust of safety systems
- **Core Motivators:** Practical clarity on what matters most, Legitimacy with crews, Respect from workforce, Operational stability
- **Dream State:** Focus on critical risks (less noise), Clear permission to stop work, Engaged workforce that speaks up

## Implications

What do these findings mean for product decisions?

### Product Development Implications

- **Fatality prevention must be the organizing principle** - not "zero harm" or general safety improvement
- **Systems thinking over individual blame** - design for human fallibility, not perfect compliance
- **Data must connect to action** - dashboards and reports are meaningless without visible follow-through
- **Credibility is fragile** - one tolerated unsafe act or broken promise undoes progress
- **Leadership visibility matters** - executives need tools that make SIF risk as visible as production risk
- **Non-blame culture enables truth** - design systems that reward speaking up, not compliance policing

### Messaging Implications

- **Lead with fatality prevention** - not general safety metrics or compliance
- **Address the authority gap** - show how tools give EHS professionals leverage with leadership
- **Prove it works in reality** - not just on paper or in demos
- **Position as enabler, not cop** - systems that help workers, not catch them
- **Make risk visible early** - executives need to see fatal-risk degradation before it's too late
- **Celebrate problems, not hide them** - issues are learning opportunities, not failures

### Sales and Marketing Implications

- **Speak to the real problem** - preventing catastrophic events in systems they don't fully control
- **Address skepticism** - acknowledge failed systems (Enablon, INX) and prove this is different
- **Show transformation** - use customer VoC to demonstrate the shift from market cynicism to customer clarity
- **Executive messaging** - "Most organisations unintentionally optimise for productivity because they cannot see fatal-risk degradation in time"
- **Primary persona messaging** - "Will this finally help me do what I'm accountable for - or is it another system I have to manage that won't change outcomes?"

## Notes

This research provides comprehensive Voice of Customer insights from three target personas (Safety System Owner, Accountable Executive, Operational Leader), general market feedback, and customer transformation evidence.

**Key Distinction:** This research focuses on buyer psychology, market sentiment, and customer transformation - complementing the existing Jobs-to-Be-Done personas research which focuses on platform user needs and design implications.

**Critical Insight:** The contrast between market VoC (scepticism, defensiveness, fatigue) and customer VoC (clarity, alignment, confidence) demonstrates the transformation customers experience after using Forwood. This is powerful evidence for sales and marketing.

The research reveals that customers are not trying to "improve safety metrics" or "roll out software" - they're trying to prevent catastrophic events in systems they don't fully control, and stop being the shock absorber between human failure and organisational denial. This is the real problem we must speak to.
